RingFence

Abuse rings are invisible one transaction at a time. RingFence builds an identity graph over payment traffic, finds the collusion inside it, and then tries hard to prove the graph was not worth it.

The problem

A single card-testing payment is a ₹5 failed charge. There is nothing in that row to learn from.

There is everything to learn from the fact that the same device tried two hundred of them. Three loss classes look unrelated in a dashboard and are the same thing underneath.

Validated twice, with opposite outcomes

Synthetic corpus

458k payments, ring-labelled, benign confounders planted

+24.5% recall at precision 0.95. Survives a permutation test, 20% of fraud going unlabelled, and six leakage checks.

IEEE-CIS (Vesta)

590k real card-not-present transactions, 3.5% fraud

No measurable difference. 0.4546 ± 0.0043 against 0.4561 ± 0.0041 across five seeds. That gap is 0.4 pooled standard deviations.

A single-seed run had the graph ahead by +2.9% and I nearly wrote it up. Refitting across five seeds showed run-to-run variance was three times the size of the effect. There is now a seedstudy command so no claim in the repo can be made without clearing its own noise floor.

I then offered an explanation for the null, that IEEE-CIS fraud is single-actor rather than collusive, and tested it. If true, the advantage should grow with how much linked-account structure a payment sits in. Bucketed by cluster size, there is no trend, and the only significant result is the graph doing worse on small clusters. The explanation was withdrawn.

The graph produces a large, verified improvement on a benchmark whose ring structure I constructed. It produces no measurable improvement on real data, and a targeted search for the subgroup where it should have helped found nothing. Why it does not transfer is unresolved.

How it works

Three decisions carry the result.

1. Shared identifiers are graded, not binary

Most identifier sharing is innocent: families share cards, a carrier NAT puts 400 customers on one IP, an apartment block shares a delivery address. Edge weight is prior[type] × log(N/df(v)) / log(N), so a card on 3 accounts is loud and an IP on 400 is silent. Hub identifiers past a per-type cap are dropped outright rather than down-weighted, because a carrier NAT block carries no information at any weight and keeping it injects an 80,000-edge clique.

2. Cohort synchrony separates a drop address from an apartment block

Both are twenty accounts on one address, structurally identical in a graph. What separates them is when the accounts were created: a building's residents signed up over years, a mule cohort signed up the same week. Without this feature the graph actively hurt bust-out recall.

3. Resolve by identifier, not by customer

A mule's first payment has no history, so a customer-keyed lookup returns nothing for exactly the transactions that matter most. Resolving through the drop address it ships to lifted fraud coverage from ~40% to ~60% and flipped the ablation from −4.6% to +23.9%.

The engineering log

Five times this build produced a number that looked fine and was wrong. All five are in FINDINGS.md.

  1. A PR-AUC of 0.99 meant the benchmark was broken, not that the model was good. Every ring account was freshly minted and almost every honest account was aged, so account age alone separated them.
  2. The graph made the model worse at first. Snapshots ran on a 5-day stride and card-testing rings live 2 days, so the graph block became a tenure signal that anti-correlated with fraud.
  3. Louvain at resolution above 1 shatters cliques into singletons, and a ring sharing one drop address is a clique. The tell was a recall cliff at exactly the code's clustering cutoff.
  4. A pruned identifier was still resolving payments into clusters. Every payment carrying a gmail address inherited whatever cluster gmail touched: 100% of rows "in a cluster", median cluster of 153 accounts. Fixing it raised the headline.
  5. I tested my own explanation for the null and withdrew it. The comfortable story did not survive contact with the data.

Every alert is contestable

A score is not actionable, and "the model said so" is not something to put in front of a merchant whose payment was blocked. Each alert carries a reason and the evidence behind it. Reasons come from group occlusion rather than SHAP: each feature group is replaced with what a normal customer looks like and the payment re-scored, which yields a real counterfactual on the real model.

Defense only

The track disqualifies anything offense-capable, and this is enforced by architecture rather than promised. The service consumes transaction records and emits risk scores and evidence. Every route is GET. There is no endpoint that blocks a payment, issues a refund, or mutates an account. The generator produces labelled abuse patterns for evaluation only: card fingerprints are opaque random tokens with no BIN structure, and no real cardholder data enters the system.
